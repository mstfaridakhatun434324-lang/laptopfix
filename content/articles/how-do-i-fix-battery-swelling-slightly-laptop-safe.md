First, the direct answer: a slightly swollen battery isn't an emergency this second, but it is a problem with a deadline. The puffiness means gas is building up inside the cells, and that process doesn't reverse. Your job now is to stay safe, protect your data, and get the battery replaced before "slightly" becomes "seriously."

Here's exactly what to do, in the order that keeps you safe. One rule underlies everything below: never puncture, squeeze, or try to "drain the swelling away." The gas is sealed inside the cells, and pressing on a swollen pouch is how a manageable situation becomes a fire. Handling is about minimizing stress on the pack until it's replaced.

## Why this happens

- **Electrolyte breakdown** — Over time (or with heat/abuse), the liquid electrolyte inside lithium cells decomposes into gas. The sealed pouch has nowhere to vent, so it swells.
- **Chronic heat** — Laptops that run hot for years swell batteries far earlier; heat is the great accelerator of electrolyte breakdown.
- **Sitting at 100%** — Batteries held at full charge for months degrade faster, and swelling is one form that degradation takes.
- **Over-discharge damage** — A battery left dead for months can swell when finally recharged, as damaged chemistry reacts badly.
- **Manufacturing defects** — Occasionally a cell is just flawed; these tend to swell within the first year or two.
- **Physical damage** — A drop or crush that dents the pack can trigger slow swelling weeks later.

## How to fix it

There is no DIY fix for swelling — the "fix" is safe handling plus replacement. Work through these in order.

### Fix 1: Confirm it's actually swelling

Make sure you're not chasing a warped chassis or a loose panel.

1. Place the laptop on a flat table and look along the bottom — a swollen battery pushes the panel out, creating a wobble or visible bulge.
2. Check the keyboard deck and trackpad — swelling often pushes the trackpad up, making clicks feel stiff or uneven.
3. Look for new gaps between the bottom panel and the chassis.
4. If the trackpad suddenly feels raised or the laptop rocks on a flat surface, that's swelling until proven otherwise.

### Fix 2: Back up your data immediately

Do this today, not this week.

1. Copy important files to an external drive or cloud storage right now.
2. A swollen battery can force a sudden shutdown if it shifts or the protection circuit trips — don't gamble unsaved work.
3. If the laptop is your only machine, prioritize documents, photos, and anything irreplaceable first. A full disk image to an external drive is even better if you have the storage — it captures settings and application data too, so a sudden battery-forced shutdown costs you nothing.

### Fix 3: Change how you use it until replacement

Reduce risk while you arrange the swap.

1. **Don't charge it unattended** — charge only while you're present and awake; unplug when done.
2. Stop running heavy workloads that heat the battery — no gaming or rendering on a swelling pack.
3. Don't press on the swollen area, stack things on the laptop, or carry it in a tight bag that squeezes the chassis.
4. Use it on a hard, non-flammable surface (desk, not bed).

### Fix 4: Stop the behaviors that accelerate swelling

Slow the progression while you wait for the replacement.

1. If your laptop has a charge limit feature, enable it and cap at 60–70% — lower voltage means slower gas generation.
2. Keep the laptop cool: clean vents, elevate for airflow, avoid hot rooms.
3. Don't deep-discharge it — keep it in the mid-range, which is gentlest on stressed cells.
4. Related: [laptop battery overheating fixes](/articles/how-to-fix-laptop-battery-overheating-step-by-step/) help keep temperatures down in the meantime.

### Fix 5: Arrange a professional replacement

This is the actual fix — everything else is risk management.

1. Check warranty first: swelling within the warranty period is often covered — contact [Dell](https://www.dell.com/support), [HP](https://support.hp.com/us-en), or [Lenovo](https://support.lenovo.com/us/en/).
2. Out of warranty: use the manufacturer or a reputable repair shop. Battery replacement on sealed laptops requires opening the chassis — not a first-time DIY job.
3. Insist on a genuine or manufacturer-approved battery. Counterfeit packs are the ones that make headlines.
4. Don't delay more than a couple of weeks — "slightly" swollen has a habit of becoming "very" swollen without warning.

### Fix 6: Know the red lines — when it becomes urgent

Slight swelling is manageable; these signs mean act today.

1. **Rapid growth** — visibly more swollen than last week.
2. **Heat** — the battery area hot without heavy use.
3. **Smell** — sweet, chemical, or metallic odor from the vents.
4. **Hissing or popping sounds.**
5. If any of these appear: power down, unplug, move the laptop to a non-flammable surface away from people, and call a repair shop — don't transport a hot, hissing battery in a car cabin.

### Fix 7: Dispose of the old battery properly

The removed swollen battery is still hazardous.

1. Don't throw it in the trash — lithium batteries cause garbage-truck fires.
2. Tape over the connector/terminals with electrical tape.
3. Take it to a battery recycling point or electronics store with a battery drop-off.
4. Store it in a cool, dry place in a non-flammable container until drop-off — not in a drawer full of papers.

## Closing

A slightly swollen battery is your laptop waving a yellow flag: not a fire today, but a replacement soon. Back up, baby it (cool, mid-charge, never unattended charging), and get a genuine replacement fitted within days to weeks.

When the new battery goes in, start its life right: enable the charge limit on day one, keep the vents clean, and avoid the heat habits that swelled the last one. Batteries are consumables, but the difference between a pack that swells at 18 months and one that lasts four years is almost entirely how it's treated. Swelling never gets better on its own — but handled promptly and calmly, it's a routine repair, not a disaster.
